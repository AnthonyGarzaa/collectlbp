Vib-Ripple (2004)

Five years after Vib-Ribbon, NanaOn-Sha brought Vibri back for a PlayStation 2 sequel: Vib-Ripple (ビブリップル), released in Japan on May 27, 2004 and, like its predecessor, designed by Masaya Matsuura. It was the third entry in the studio's loosely connected "Ribbon" line — after Vib-Ribbon (1999) and the word-based Mojib-Ribbon (2003) — and it kept the series' habit of building play out of media the player already owns. This time the raw material wasn't music. It was photographs.

Where the first game turned an audio CD into a scribbled line, Vib-Ripple turns a picture into a trampoline. It ships with 60 built-in photos, but the real point is importing your own: you can load images over USB from a digital camera or cellphone, or pull them in over the network, and the game scales each one to a 256×256 playfield. That photo then becomes a taut, bouncy membrane stretched across the screen.

Vibri — still the same vector-drawn rabbit — lands on the picture, and button presses send ripples rolling across its surface to bounce her around. The goal is to shake loose the flat little "Peta Characters" hidden in the image and collect them before time runs out, with an on-screen icon hinting at which part of the photo to work. Lurking among them are hostile creatures called Boonchies; touch one and Vibri de-evolves down the familiar chain — rabbit to frog to worm — and a full collapse ends the run. Bounce well enough and she powers up into Super Vibri, briefly stunning the Boonchies and exposing whatever they were hiding.

The result looks nothing like Vib-Ribbon's stark white line — it's bright, busy, and built from whatever snapshots you feed it — but the DNA is unmistakable: hand the level design to the player and reward attention over score-chasing. Vib-Ripple never left Japan, and reviewers were split, with some finding it "too different" from the original while still praising the endless replay value of the import feature. For Vibri fans it remains the rabbit's last starring role to date, and one of the purest expressions of NanaOn-Sha's player-as-author philosophy.
